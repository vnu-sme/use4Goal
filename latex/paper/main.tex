\section{Introduction}
\label{sect:intro}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

%----------------------------------------------
%-------- Research context & problems ---------
%----------------------------------------------
Modern software engineering is increasingly shaped by the need to deliver reliable systems in the face of high complexity. Model-Driven Engineering (MDE) elevates models to first-class artifacts, supporting early validation and automated generation of code and documentation \cite{KHALIL2018145}. Within this paradigm, the Unified Modeling Language (UML) provides expressive constructs to model structural and behavioral aspects of systems \cite{booch2005unified}, while the Object Constraint Language (OCL) introduces a formal textual mechanism to specify constraints and business rules beyond graphical representations \cite{warmer1998object}. The integration of UML and OCL enables a comprehensive specification
of requirements while supporting automated analysis of their consistency and correctness. However, OCL is inherently limited to evaluating either individual system states or the pre- and postconditions of operations,
which makes it unsuitable for specifying temporal constraints and event-driven behaviors. This gap prevents practitioners from capturing requirements such as ordering of operations, bounded occurrences, or liveness properties, which are crucial for reactive and time-sensitive systems.
%----------------------------------------------
%------------- State of the art ---------------
%----------------------------------------------

Several extensions have been proposed to overcome these limitations. Temporal OCL (TOCL) \cite{kanso2014specification,ziemann2003ocl}  extends OCL with temporal operators inspired by temporal logics such as
Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) and Computation Tree Logic (CTL), thereby allowing properties like \textit{always}, \textit{eventually}, and \textit{until}
to be expressed directly in OCL syntax. While these proposals enhance reasoning about state evolution, they remain limited: TOCL lacks explicit constructs for event handling
and operation counting, and most temporal verification approaches depend on specialized tools that are not well integrated into the UML/OCL toolchain \cite{hilkenTransformationUMLOCL2014}. Hence, there remains a gap between temporal specification and practical verification in MDE.

%----------------------------------------------
%---------------- Our proposal ----------------
%----------------------------------------------
We propose TOCL+, an extension of OCL that supports both temporal and event-driven properties while preserving the familiar syntax of OCL. TOCL+ introduces constructs such as \texttt{isCalled} for operation events, \texttt{becomesTrue} for state changes, and bounded quantifiers for event frequencies. Verification is based on the film-stripping technique \cite{hilkenTransformationUMLOCL2014}, which encodes dynamic behavior as sequences of snapshots and operation calls. TOCL+ constraints are systematically translated into standard OCL over filmstrip models, allowing verification using existing tools such as the USE Model Validator \cite{gogolla2018achieving}, without the need for specialized temporal logic engines.

This paper makes the following key contributions: (1)~A language extension (TOCL+) that enhances OCL with temporal operators, event constructs, and bounded quantifiers. (2)~A systematic translation methodology based on filmtripping to verify temporal properties. (3)~A prototype plugin integrated into the USE environment to support verification. (4)~An evaluation through a case study that demonstrates the feasibility of the approach.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sect:motivation} reviews the necessary background on UML, OCL, TOCL and filmstripping. Section~\ref{sect:overview} gives an overview of our approach, introduces the TOCL+ language and outlines the transformation approach for verification. Section~\ref{sect:toolSupport} describes the tool support and discusses the applicability of our method. Section~\ref{sect:relatedWork} reviews related work. Finally, Section~\ref{sect:conclusion} concludes the paper and discusses the directions for future work.

Together, these examples illustrate how OCL can formally express system rules, operation semantics, and cross-object consistency, while also revealing the language’s limitations in expressing more complex temporal or quantitative properties.

For instance, articulating requirements such as “if a crossing request is made, the next operation must be a call to \texttt{switchPedLight()}” exceeds the capabilities of standard OCL, which cannot enforce the sequence or timing of events. Additionally, OCL lacks mechanisms to express constraints involving the frequency or history of operations, such as tracking how many times an action occurs within a specified interval. These gaps hinder the formal specification and verification of systems where time and event dependencies are critical.


However, OCL, based on first-order logic, lacks native support for specifying temporal properties~\cite{lail2022TOCL}. Some approaches rely on non-UML formalisms, requiring the transformation of UML models into external verification frameworks, which are not well aligned with MDE. Therefore, extending OCL with temporal constructs is a more suitable solution. Among the notable efforts in this direction, TOCL~\cite{ziemann2003ocl} extends OCL to address its core limitation: the inability to express temporal properties that span multiple system states. While OCL is effective for invariants and pre- and postconditions within a single snapshot, it cannot describe evolving behavior in reactive or real-time systems.

TOCL enriches OCL with temporal operators inspired by LTL~\cite{rozier2011linear}. Constraints are evaluated over a  infinite sequence of states and organizes its temporal operators into two categories~\cite{ziemann2003ocl}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Future Operators}: These include \texttt{next e} (true if $e$ holds in the next state), \texttt{always e} (true if $e$ holds in the current and all subsequent states), and \texttt{sometime e} (true if $e$ holds in the current or at least one future state).
    \item \textbf{Past Operators}: These include \texttt{previous e} (true if $e$ was true in the previous state), \texttt{alwaysPast e} (true if $e$ was true in all past states), and \texttt{sometimePast e} (true if $e$ was true in at least one past state).
\end{itemize}

These operators provide the core temporal reasoning capabilities in TOCL, allowing precise specification of how properties evolve over time, a critical feature for modeling and verifying dynamic system behavior. TOCL's ability to express temporal relations between states is particularly valuable for verifying properties in reactive and real-time systems, where actions in the system are triggered by the passage of time or by other events.


However, TOCL faces considerable limitations when specifying constraints that involve event-driven behaviors. For instance, articulating requirements such as \textit{“if a crossing request is made, the next operation must be a call to switchPedLight()”} or \textit{“detecting that an event becomes true between two states”} exceeds the capabilities of TOCL. Additionally, TOCL lacks mechanisms to capture constraints involving the precise frequency of operations, such as restricting how many times an action may occur within a specified interval. These gaps hinder the formal specification and verification of systems where quantitative control over event occurrences is critical.

To overcome these limitations, we propose extending TOCL, a version that augments OCL with temporal constraints, enabling more direct modeling of time-dependent and event-driven requirements. This raises several important questions for further research: (1)~How can TOCL be extended to capture such temporal and event-based constraints? (2)~In what way can these new constraints be translated into UML/OCL models? (3)~How can the correctness of these newly added constraints be verified?

\subsection{Filmstrip Method for Model Validation}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The model filmstripping method  \cite{hilkenTransformationUMLOCL2014}  enables efficient verification of UML/OCL models
by addressing dynamic aspects through techniques traditionally applied to static analysis. Specifically, the UML/OCL model, a so-called application model, is transformed into an equivalent model containing only invariants, referred to as the filmstrip model.
The validity of the application model can be verified by applying static verification techniques to this filmstrip model. For a better understanding, we would take a look at the \textit{TrafficLight} example above with the original model shown in Figure~\ref{fig:case-study}. 

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{backup/version0.1/images/TOCL+filmstrip.png}
  \caption{Transform TrafficLight into the filmstrip model.}
  \label{fig:filmstrip-example}
\end{figure}



 Figure~\ref{fig:filmstrip-example} shows the filmstrip model obtained from the original model \textit{TrafficLight}. Two operations of the class \texttt{TrafficLight}, \texttt{switchCarLight()} and \texttt{switchPedLight()}, correspond to two classes in the filmstrip model, \texttt{switchCarLight$\_$TrafficLightOpC} and \texttt{switchPedLight$\_$TrafficLightOpC}. A transition between two system snapshots w.r.t a call of the operations would be represented in only one corresponding snapshot in the filmstrip model. 

 \begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{backup/version0.1/images/App_Filmstrip_model.png}
  \caption{Transform Application into the filmstrip model.}
  \label{fig:app-example}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:app-example} presents the filmstrip model obtained from the application-management system. The operations of the class \texttt{System} in the original model, such as \texttt{install()}, \texttt{load()}, \texttt{run()}, and \texttt{stop()}, are transformed into the corresponding operation-classes \texttt{install$\_$SystemOpC}, \texttt{load$\_$SystemOpC}, \texttt{run$\_$SystemOpC}, and \texttt{stop$\_$SystemOpC}. Similar to the previous example, each call of an operation is represented in the filmstrip by linking two snapshots through the temporal relations \texttt{pred} and \texttt{succ}. The classes \texttt{System} and \texttt{Application} appear in every snapshot, allowing changes in attributes such as memory usage and application sets to be captured in a static form. This enables the verification of system behavior by applying standard OCL invariant checking on the filmstrip structure.

 \begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{backup/version0.1/images/BankAccount_filmstrip_model.png}
  \caption{Transform BankAccount into the filmstrip model.}

Figure~\ref{fig:bank-example} illustrates the filmstrip model derived from the \textit{BankAccount} case study. The operations of the class \texttt{BankAccount}, specifically \texttt{deposit()} and \texttt{withdraw()}, are transformed into the corresponding operation-classes \texttt{deposit$\_$BankAccountOpC} and \texttt{withdraw$\_$BankAccountOpC}. Consistent with the previous examples, the execution of a transaction is represented in the filmstrip by linking two snapshots—representing the pre-state and post-state—through the temporal relations \texttt{pred} and \texttt{succ}. Key attributes, such as the account \texttt{balance}, are maintained within each snapshot. This transformation allows dynamic constraints, such as preventing a balance from becoming negative or ensuring data consistency during transactions, to be formulated and verified as static invariants on the filmstrip model.

\subsection{TOCL+: An Extension for OCL Temporal Properties}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% 

While TOCL extended OCL with temporal operators for reasoning
about past and future system states, it faced significant limitations, particularly in expressing event-driven behaviors and bounded existence properties. TOCL could only track state changes, not actual event occurrences (e.g., the number of times an operation is invoked). These gaps were addressed with the introduction of TOCL+.

\begin{figure}[htbp]	
\vspace{-.5em}
\begin{center}
	% \includegraphics[scale=0.8]
	\includegraphics[scale=0.6]
    {backup/version0.1/images/TOCL+TOCL+ (1).png}
        \vspace{-0.7em}
	\begin{center}		
		\caption{Example of a state transition.}
            \label{fig:stateTransition}
	\end{center}	
	\vspace{-2.7em}
\end{center}
\end{figure}

TOCL+ extends TOCL by incorporating event constructs and bounded existence operators, which significantly expand its expressive power for dynamic system behaviors. The Figure~\ref{fig:stateTransition} shows a state-transition sequence where states are annotated with condition \texttt{P} and transitions are triggered by operation calls \texttt{op}. This serves to illustrate the core features supported in TOCL+.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Event Constructs}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{isCalled}: Detects the invocation of a specific operation on an object, representing the atomic transition from pre-state to post-state.
        \item \texttt{becomesTrue}: Detects when a condition \texttt{P} changes from false to true between two consecutive states.
    \end{itemize}
    
    \item \textbf{Bounded Existence Properties}: TOCL+ introduces bounded quantifiers for counting events:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{isCalled(...)} at most $k$ times: Restricts the operation to be called no more than $k$ times.
        \item \texttt{isCalled(...)} $k$ times: Specifies that the operation must be called exactly $k$ times.
        \item \texttt{isCalled(...)} at least $k$ times: Ensures the operation is called at least $k$ times.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

There are two main benefits of our extension. First, these new capabilities enable TOCL+ to express properties that were previously inexpressible in TOCL, such as ensuring that an operation is invoked a specified number of times or that a system event occurs within a given range of system states. Importantly, this extension preserves the familiar OCL syntax, ensuring that TOCL+ remains accessible to practitioners already accustomed to OCL. Second, the introduction of event-based constructs in TOCL+ allows for a more intuitive specification of reactive systems and real-time applications, where behaviors are driven by the occurrence of specific events (e.g., operation calls, state changes). By adding bounded existence operators, TOCL+ supports verification of safety properties like \textit{"an operation is called at most once"} or \textit{"a system transition occurs exactly three times"}.
\begin{lstcodeplainssm}{EBNF Grammar}{lst:grammarebnf}
primaryExp[Environment env] returns [ASTOclExpression ast]
  : literalExp[$env]
  | varExp[$env]
  | callExp[$env, null]
  | ifExp[$env]
  | toclOperatorExpression[$env]
  | LPAREN oclExpression[$env] RPAREN
  | events[$env]
  {
    $ast = $events.ast;
  };

events[Environment env] returns [ASTEvent ast]
  : isCalledEvent[$env] { $ast = $isCalledEvent.ast; }
  | becomesTrueEvent[$env] { \$ast = \$becomesTrueEvent.ast; };

isCalledEvent[Environment env] returns [ASTEvent ast]
  : 'isCalled' LPAREN eventOp[$env] RPAREN bounds[$env]?
  {
    $_ast = new ASTEvent();
  };

bounds[Environment env]
  : quantif=('at least' | 'at most')? n=NATURAL_N 'times';

eventOp[Environment env]
  : simpleName LPAREN parameters[$env]? RPAREN;

becomesTrueEvent[Environment env] returns [ASTEvent ast]
  : 'becomesTrue' LPAREN e=binaryOperationExp[$env] RPAREN
  {
    $_ast = new ASTEvent();
  };
  
\end{lstcodeplainssm}

Listing \ref{lst:grammarebnf} shows the formal EBNF as the  Grammar of two new events constructed types extending the formal grammar that was built by Lail et al. \cite{lail2022TOCL}. The formal grammar specification serves two principal functions. It delivers a rigorous definition of the TOCL+ syntax, thereby complementing the semantic foundation introduced in the preceding section. In addition, it facilitates the systematic translation of TOCL+ specifications into standard OCL constraints, which are subsequently employed in our verification framework described in the next section.

Listing~\ref{lst:tocl+Properties} presents representative TOCL+ constraints for the \textit{TrafficLight} system. They capture temporal and event-driven requirements, such as detecting when specific operations are called and when an OCL expression \texttt{P} changes from false to true. These examples highlight how TOCL+ extends TOCL with additional constructs to specify dynamic and event-dependent system behavior more precisely.

\begin{lstcodeplainssm}{TOCL+ properties for Application} {lst:ApplicationProperties}
context System
-- An application must be loaded before it is run
inv safety1:
    self.runningApps->notEmpty() implies
    self.runningApps->forAll(app |
        isCalled(run(app : Application)) implies
        sometimePast isCalled(load(app : Application))

inv safety2:
    self.runningApps->notEmpty() implies
    self.runningApps->forAll(app |
        isCalled(run(app : Application)) implies
        sometime isCalled(install()) since isCalled(load(app : Application))
    )
-- Each application can be loaded at most once in the past
inv safety3:
    self.installedApps->notEmpty() implies
    self.installedApps->forAll(app |

    self.loadedApps->notEmpty() implies

        sometime isCalled(install())
    )

context Application
-- Once an application becomes loaded, it must eventually become installed
inv livenessBecomesTrue:
    self.system.loadedApps->includes(self) implies
    sometime becomesTrue(self.system.installedApps->includes(self))


\end{lstcodeplainssm}

\label{alg::toclToOcl}
\KwIn{An UML/TOCL+ Application Model}
\KwOut{SAT(witness object diagram) | UNSAT}

Transform the application model into a filmstrip model using the USE Filmstrip plugin\;
Translate TOCL+ constraints into the corresponding OCL constraints over the transformed filmstrip model by applying a set of translation rules on TOCL+ constraints\;
Verify the filmstrip model with the translated OCL constraints and additional constraints by model finding\;

\If{a model (object diagram) is found}{
  \Return SATISFIABLE with the object diagram\;
}
\Else{
  \Return UNSATISFIABLE\;
}

\subsection{Verifying TOCL+ Properties}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Algorithm~\ref{alg::toclToOcl} outlines the process of verifying TOCL+ constraints by determining whether a witness model instance exists that satisfies all specified conditions. This task can be carried out using UML/OCL model finders, such as the USE Model Validator~\cite{kuhlmannUMLOCLRelational2012} or UMLtoCSP~\cite{cabot2014verification}. These tools translate the structural and behavioral aspects of the model, along with its constraints, into relational logic~\cite{torlakKodkodRelationalModel2007}, and then rely on constraint solvers to systematically explore a user-defined finite search space for object diagrams that fulfill all invariants. A key step in this process is defining appropriate lower and upper bounds for objects, associations, and attribute values, as this greatly influences the efficiency of model finding.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sect:evaluative}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

To position our approach, we compare TOCL+ with two major classes of state-of-the-art verification frameworks. The first includes traditional temporal model checkers such as Spin~\cite{holzmann2004spin} and NuSMV~\cite{cimatti2002nusmv}, which provide exhaustive verification of low-level temporal logics like LTL and CTL. Although highly powerful, these tools rely on distinct specification formalisms (e.g., Promela~\cite{holzmann2004spin}, SMV~\cite{cimatti2002nusmv}), forcing designers to manually translate UML/OCL models into external representations. This translation process introduces a semantic gap between design and verification artifacts, is prone to errors, and requires substantial expertise in temporal logic—posing a steep learning curve for MDE practitioners. Moreover, expressing high-level, domain-oriented properties, such as “an operation is called at most three times,” is cumbersome in LTL and often requires explicit encoding of auxiliary state variables, reducing clarity and maintainability.

The second class comprises UML/OCL-based verification tools such as UMLtoCSP~\cite{cabot2014verification}, which employ constraint programming or relational logic. While these approaches preserve the UML/OCL semantics, they generally lack native mechanisms to express temporal relationships across multiple states or capture operation-call events, thereby limiting their expressiveness for dynamic system behavior.

Standard OCL is unable to capture temporal dependencies, as it only evaluates invariants over a single system state. A liveness property like inv TL1: self.requested = true implies sometime isCalled(switchPedLight()) cannot be represented. Our TOCL+ approach bridges this exact expressiveness gap. By extending OCL with high-level operators such as \textit{sometime}, \textit{isCalled}, and \textit{becomesTrue}, it enables designers to specify complex temporal and event-driven properties directly within the UML/OCL environment. Implemented as a plugin for the USE tool, TOCL+ performs verification through bounded model finding, eliminating the need for external model transformation. Consequently, while traditional model checkers like Spin and NuSMV achieve higher scalability for pure logical verification, TOCL+  prioritizes accessibility, practical efficiency, and native consistency with MDE workflows, making temporal property verification both expressive and usable in real-world modeling environments.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Discussion}
\label{sect:discussion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Although $TOCL+$ demonstrates the feasibility of integrating temporal property verification into a standard UML/OCL environment, the approach exhibits several important limitations concerning expressiveness, scalability, and the underlying verification assumptions.

First, the filmstrip abstraction limits temporal expressiveness. By discretizing system evolution into ordered snapshots, the current approach cannot natively express quantitative real-time requirements. For example, a constraint such as "an operation must be invoked within five seconds" is not supported. Future work could address this by extending the filmstrip metamodel with explicit timestamp attributes, allowing time and duration constraints to be encoded as standard OCL comparisons.

Second, the reliance on bounded model finding impacts scalability. Translating $TOCL+$ into standard OCL and applying model finding exposes the approach to state-space explosion as the filmstrip depth or model complexity increases. This limitation is coupled with the usability challenge of manually configuring search bounds—a process requiring expertise, as restrictive bounds can conceal counterexamples while permissive bounds increase resource usage. Consequently, $TOCL+$ is intended for early fault detection within bounded scenarios, not as a replacement for high-performance, exhaustive model checkers.

Finally, the method's validity depends entirely on the translation fidelity. The soundness and completeness of the verification results rely on the correctness of the $TOCL+$ to OCL translation rules and the fidelity of the underlying model finder. To strengthen internal validity, these translation rules must be formally specified and independently validated. We also plan to publish all implementation artifacts and benchmark suites to enable independent replication, which will increase confidence in the method's generality and robustness.

Taken together, these limitations identify a focused research agenda: extending the filmstrip model for real-time constraints, quantitatively benchmarking scalability, automating search bound selection, and formally validating the translation toolchain.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Related work}
\label{sect:relatedWork}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Various methods have been proposed to address the challenge of specifying and verifying temporal properties in UML models. Al Lail et al \cite{ailTPV2025} introduced a technique similar to ours, converting application models into Snapshot Transition Models (STM) and using TOCL for specification. However, their approach still inherits the limitations of TOCL when dealing with event-based properties. Kanso and Taha \cite{kanso2014specification} and Dadeau et al. \cite{dadeauTemporalPattern2019} explored pattern-based specification methods, implementing these patterns in Eclipse, but without providing verification capabilities or focusing on model-based testing instead of comprehensive verification. Baresi et al. \cite{baresiMADES2015} presented the MADES approach, which integrates multiple heavyweight formalisms for verifying embedded systems but requires a steep learning curve and advanced mathematical skills, making it difficult for many UML designers. Similarly, Combemale et al. \cite{combemalrPropertyDriven2008} used a temporal extension of OCL translated into Petri nets, which necessitates designers learning additional formalisms to interpret the verification results. Meyers et al. \cite{Meyers2014} supported temporal property verification in domain-specific modeling through a set of five languages, relying on LTL and the Spin model checker, creating a separation between specification and verification languages. Hilken and Gogolla \cite{hilken2016} introduced a filmstripping approach similar to our verification technique, but without supporting high-level temporal property specification. In contrast, our approach combines specification and verification within the well-known OCL context, removing the need for learning additional formalisms.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sect:conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This paper has presented a comprehensive approach for verifying temporal properties in model-driven engineering environments, with a particular focus on UML/OCL models. This research encompasses three core contributions. First, it introduces two time-determining concepts that extend the TOCL syntax, thereby enhancing the expressiveness and verification capabilities for temporal constraints in UML/OCL models. Second, a transformer from TOCL+ to OCL has been developed, allowing temporal specifications to be directly incorporated into existing verification tools. Third, a practical verification tool
has been implemented and demonstrated, evidencing the
effectiveness and applicability of the proposed approach
through concrete scenarios.

% By integrating the USE Validator with the TOCL+ and Filmstrip plugins, the proposed framework enables systematic and automated analysis of time-dependent behaviors and state transitions. The introduction of two novel time-determining concepts, the development of an automated converter from TOCL+ to OCL, and the implementation of a practical verification tool collectively represent significant advancements in the expressiveness and applicability of temporal constraint validation.

Through concrete scenarios such as the \textit{TrafficLight} example, the effectiveness of the method has been demonstrated, offering enhanced traceability and transparency in the verification process. Despite certain limitations regarding configuration management and the expressiveness of temporal logic, the results establish a robust and extensible foundation for future research and tool development. Subsequent work may further refine the framework by supporting more sophisticated temporal constraints and improving automation, thereby advancing the practical utility of temporal property verification in complex software systems.
